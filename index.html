What a foreign national is permitted to establish

Spain's foreign investment regime is liberalized. A U.S. person or company may own 100% of a Spanish company, sit on its board, and act as sole director. There is no local-partner requirement and no local-director requirement. What you owe the state is a declaration, not permission, with one significant exception, noted in the screening flag below.

S.L. (Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada)

The private limited company, and the default answer for roughly 95% of U.S. entrants. Limited liability, ownership in participaciones (not freely tradable shares), one or more shareholders, flexible governance.

  • Minimum capital: €1 since the 2022 Crea y Crece reform. But below €3,000 you must route 20% of annual profit to a reserve until capital plus reserve reaches €3,000, and shareholders are jointly liable for the shortfall on liquidation. Fund it at €3,000+ anyway: banks, landlords and customers read a €1 company as a shell.
  • Sole-shareholder form (S.L.U.) is standard for a wholly owned U.S. subsidiary; it simply must be flagged in the registry and on invoices.

S.A. (Sociedad Anónima)

The public limited company. €60,000 minimum capital, 25% paid in at incorporation. Freely transferable shares, mandatory independent valuation of non-cash contributions, heavier formalities.

Choose it only if you plan to bring in Spanish co-investors, need share classes for an eventual listing, or operate in a sector that requires it (banking, insurance, some concessions). Otherwise it is cost without benefit.

Branch (Sucursal)

A registered extension of the U.S. parent. No separate legal personality: the parent is directly liable for everything the branch does, and the parent's own accounts must be filed with the Spanish registry and become public.

Taxed on Spanish-source income at the same 25% rate as a subsidiary, so the old tax argument for branches has largely evaporated. Still used by banks, insurers and construction firms bidding single large projects.

Representative office (Oficina de representación)

No legal personality, no capital, and critically no revenue-generating activity: market research, liaison and promotion only. The moment it negotiates or signs contracts it risks creating a taxable permanent establishment retroactively.

Cheap for a 6–12 month scouting phase. It still needs a tax ID and must register any staff with Social Security.

Joint venture and consortium structures

Options range from a co-owned S.L. to the U.T.E. (Unión Temporal de Empresas), a tax-transparent temporary consortium used heavily for public works and infrastructure bids, and often the only practical route into Spanish public procurement. There is also the A.I.E. for ongoing cooperation between firms.

What you would not normally use

  • Autónomo (sole trader): unlimited personal liability, individuals only.
  • Sociedad Civil / Comunidad de Bienes: partnership forms with personal liability.
  • Cooperativa: member-owned, regionally regulated, occasionally relevant in agri-food.
FDI screening applies to you specifically because you are not European. Under Article 7 bis of Law 19/2003, a non-EU/EFTA investor acquiring 10% or more of a Spanish company in a strategic sector needs prior government authorization. Not a notification: an approval, with a statutory answer window of three to six months. Strategic sectors include critical infrastructure (energy, transport, water, health, communications), dual-use and defense technology, semiconductors, AI and robotics, aerospace, critical raw materials, and media. The regime also catches very large deals regardless of sector. Greenfield builds are generally outside it, acquisitions frequently are not. Screen this in week one, because it sits on the critical path ahead of everything else in this brief.

The always-applicable legal layer

  • Four levels of law, not two. EU regulation, Spanish national law, the 17 Autonomous Communities, and the municipality. A U.S. firm that thinks in "federal and state" will miss the two layers that actually control its permits.
  • Notarial and registry formalism. Incorporation, capital changes, powers of attorney, property transfers and most corporate acts require a public deed before a notario and entry in a public registry. This is not optional paperwork; an unregistered act is often simply ineffective against third parties.
  • GDPR plus Spain's own LOPDGDD. The Spanish DPA (AEPD) is among the most active enforcers in Europe. Employee monitoring, CCTV, cookie banners and biometric time clocks all draw attention.
  • Mandatory internal whistleblowing channel (Law 2/2023) for any employer with 50+ workers, registered with the authorities and with a named system manager.
  • Commercial agents are protected by statute. Law 12/1992 gives a terminated agent a goodwill indemnity of up to one year's average commission. It cannot be waived in advance, and Spanish courts have extended similar reasoning to some exclusive distributors. U.S. firms routinely sign agency agreements in Spain without pricing this in.
Because you chose: Office

For an office the structural question is narrow: S.L. subsidiary or branch? Take the S.L. It ring-fences U.S. parent liability, keeps the parent's financials out of the Spanish public registry, and is the structure Spanish landlords, banks and enterprise customers expect to contract with.

Because you chose: Sales & commercial

Watch the permanent establishment line. Under the U.S.–Spain treaty, a Spanish employee who habitually concludes contracts (or plays the principal role leading to their conclusion) creates a taxable PE for the U.S. parent even with no incorporated entity. "We'll just hire two reps first" is how firms end up filing Spanish corporate tax returns retroactively, with penalties. If they sell, incorporate.

Because you chose: R&D / engineering

Put the intellectual property ownership clause in writing and in Spanish-law form. Spain's Patents Act gives employee inventors a statutory claim to compensation for certain inventions unless the employment contract clearly assigns invention rights and the role is defined as research. Also register the entity for the R&D tax credit regime from day one. See the Tax tab.

Because you chose: Plant

Incorporate an S.L. and treat the corporate step as the easy part. Your binding constraints are environmental authorization, the municipal activity and works licences, and the electricity grid connection: all regional or local, all slower than incorporation, and all things you should open files on before you sign anything on a site.

Because you chose: 250+ people

At this scale you are a large employer in Spanish terms. Expect a works council, sector collective bargaining that binds you, a mandatory equality plan and pay register, and, if you ever restructure, a formal ERE collective dismissal process with a statutory consultation period. Budget for a Spanish employment counsel on retainer, not on call.

Because you chose: Canary Islands

The Canaries sit outside the EU VAT area and outside the EU customs territory for some purposes. Local indirect tax is IGIC (general rate 7%), not IVA, and goods moving to the mainland cross a tax frontier. That is a feature if you are serving West Africa or the Americas and a complication if the mainland is your market.

Documents you must produce in the United States first

Every one of these must be apostilled under the Hague Convention (both countries are parties, so no consular legalization is needed) and then translated by a sworn translator (traductor jurado) officially appointed by Spain's Ministry of Foreign Affairs. An ordinary certified translation will be rejected. Allow three to four weeks for this bundle alone; it is the single most common reason a Spanish incorporation slips.

  • Certificate of good standing / incorporation for the U.S. parent
  • Articles of incorporation and bylaws
  • Board resolution authorizing the Spanish investment and the incorporation
  • Notarized poder notarial: a power of attorney naming whoever will sign in Spain, drafted in Spanish civil-law terms rather than a U.S.-style POA
  • Passport copies for all directors, authorized signatories and ultimate beneficial owners
  • Beneficial ownership chart tracing to natural persons holding 25%+; banks and notaries will both ask

The incorporation sequence in Spain

  1. NIE and NIF. Every foreign individual involved needs an NIE (foreigner identity number); the U.S. parent entity needs a Spanish NIF (tax ID) via Form 036. Apply first, because this gates everything downstream and can take two to four weeks.
  2. Name clearance. A certificación negativa de denominación social from the Central Commercial Registry confirming your chosen name is free. Submit three to five candidate names.
  3. Bank account and capital deposit. Open the account, wire the capital, obtain the bank's certificado de desembolso. See the chicken-and-egg flag below.
  4. Deed of incorporation. Sign the escritura de constitución with the bylaws (estatutos sociales) before a notary, in person or by proxy under your apostilled POA.
  5. Provisional NIF for the new company from the tax agency (AEAT).
  6. Commercial Registry filing. Registration at the provincial Registro Mercantil is what actually creates the company as a legal person. Typically one to three weeks.
  7. Tax registration. Form 036 census declaration: activity codes, VAT status, the business rates tax (IAE), and, if you will trade with other EU states, VIES/ROI registration.
  8. Social Security. Obtain the employer contribution account code (Código de Cuenta de Cotización) from the TGSS, and register each employee before their first day. Registering late is a straightforward fine.
  9. Beneficial ownership declaration to the central registry (RCTIR).
  10. Corporate books legalized electronically at the registry: minute book, shareholder register, and thereafter annual accounts filed within one month of approval.
  11. Occupational risk prevention. Spanish law requires a risk-prevention arrangement (almost always a contract with an external servicio de prevención ajeno) plus a written risk assessment, before staff start work. U.S. firms consistently forget this one.
The banking chicken-and-egg. Historically you needed a company NIF to open an account, and a bank certificate to incorporate the company. Two ways through: use the Crea y Crece route in which founders declare the capital to the notary and accept joint liability for it, skipping the bank certificate entirely; or open the account with a bank that has a dedicated foreign-investor desk. Either way, treat account opening as a six-to-ten-week workstream, not a Tuesday errand. KYC on a U.S.-owned entity with a multi-tier ownership chain is genuinely slow.
Free help exists. ICEX–Invest in Spain is the national investment promotion agency and provides no-cost location, permitting and incentive support to inbound investors, as do the regional agencies: ACCIÓ (Catalonia), IVACE (Valencia), SPRI (Basque Country), IDEA (Andalusia), Invest in Madrid. They are also the fastest route to knowing which grants you qualify for before you commit to a site.

Post-incorporation, ongoing

  • Foreign investment declaration (Form D-1A) to the Investment Registry within one month of the investment
  • Quarterly and annual tax filings; annual accounts filed with the Commercial Registry
  • Monthly Social Security settlements via the SILTRA system
  • Daily working-time records retained four years (mandatory since 2019, for every employee)
  • Equality plan and pay register at 50+ employees; LGTBI protocol at 50+ employees
Because you chose: Plant

A parallel and longer permitting track runs beside all of the above:

  • Licencia de obras: municipal building permit
  • Licencia de actividad / licencia ambiental: activity and environmental licence, issued regionally or municipally depending on your classification
  • Autorización Ambiental Integrada (integrated environmental permit) if your activity falls in the IPPC annex. This is the long one, commonly 9 to 18 months
  • Environmental impact assessment where thresholds are met; water abstraction and discharge permits
  • Grid connection point (punto de conexión) from the distributor; request early, queues in industrial zones are real
  • Soil status declaration under RD 9/2005 for potentially contaminating activities, plus a baseline soil report you will badly want if you ever exit the site
  • EORI number for customs, and consider Authorised Economic Operator status if you import at volume

Plan 12–24 months from site selection to production, with the environmental permit as the critical path, not the company setup, which is a rounding error by comparison.

Because you chose: Food / agri / chemical processing

Add sector registration on top of the general permits: the Registro General Sanitario de Empresas Alimentarias (RGSEAA) for food operations, a HACCP plan, regional health authority inspection and, for chemicals, REACH and Seveso III obligations if you cross substance thresholds. Also budget for wastewater pre-treatment, because municipal discharge limits are strict and are the most common late-stage surprise in food processing projects.

Because you chose: Warehouse / distribution

Lighter environmental burden, but check whether your municipality allows a declaración responsable (self-certified opening declaration) instead of a full licence. Many do for non-classified warehousing, which can save six months. If you will run your own fleet you need a national transport operator licence (tarjeta de transporte) with capacity and financial-standing requirements. Note also that Spanish law since 2022 prohibits truck drivers from loading and unloading the goods they carry, which changes your dock staffing model.

Because you chose: Office

A pure office is usually a declaración responsable in Madrid and Barcelona rather than a full activity licence, and fit-out works below a threshold need only a minor-works communication. Realistic timeline: 6 to 10 weeks from apostilled documents in hand to a trading entity with a bank account, with the account and the NIEs as the long poles.

Because you chose: Basque Country / Navarre

These territories have their own tax administrations and their own corporate tax laws under the Concierto Económico and Convenio Económico. You file with the provincial Hacienda Foral (Bizkaia, Gipuzkoa, Álava) or Navarre, not with the national AEAT. Forms, deadlines, incentive schemes and rates all differ, and the foral R&D credits are materially more generous than the national ones. Engage a Basque or Navarrese adviser specifically; a Madrid firm will not be fluent in this.

Corporate income tax (Impuesto sobre Sociedades)

  • General rate 25% on worldwide income for a Spanish-resident company.
  • 15% for newly created companies in the first tax period with a positive base and the following one. A genuine cash-flow benefit for a startup subsidiary.
  • Reduced rates for smaller companies are phasing in under Law 7/2024, stepping down year by year for micro-enterprises (turnover under €1M) and small entities (under €10M) across 2025–2029. Confirm the current-year figure with your adviser rather than relying on a number you read once.
  • 15% minimum effective tax applies to groups with turnover of €20M+ or filing on a consolidated basis, regardless of credits.
  • Tax year normally matches the calendar year; the return is due 25 days after the six months following year end (so 25 July for a December close), with three advance payments in April, October and December.

VAT (IVA)

  • Standard 21%; reduced 10% (hospitality, transport, some foods); super-reduced 4% (staple foods, books, medicines). The Canary Islands use IGIC instead; Ceuta and Melilla use IPSI.
  • Returns are quarterly (Form 303) for most, monthly if turnover exceeds €6M or you opt into the monthly refund register (REDEME), plus an annual summary.
  • SII real-time reporting. Monthly filers must transmit invoice records to the tax agency within four days of issue. This is an ERP integration project, not an accounting task, so scope it early.
  • Intra-EU supplies need a valid VIES registration. Expect the first VIES application to be refused or queried; the tax agency vets foreign-controlled applicants hard and may demand evidence of real establishment.

Withholding tax and the U.S.–Spain treaty

Domestic withholding is 19% on dividends, interest and royalties paid abroad. The 2013 Protocol to the U.S.–Spain income tax treaty, in force since late 2019, is unusually generous and is a real reason to hold the Spanish entity directly from the U.S.:

  • Dividends: 0% where the U.S. parent has held 80%+ for 12 months and meets the limitation-on-benefits test; 5% at 10%+ ownership; 15% otherwise.
  • Interest: 0%. Royalties: 0%.
  • Capital gains on shares are generally taxable only in the seller's state, with a real-property exception.
The treaty is not self-executing. To get the reduced rate you need a current U.S. Form 6166 residency certificate and must satisfy the limitation-on-benefits article. Miss the paperwork and 19% is withheld at source; reclaiming it takes months. Also run the U.S. side (GILTI, Subpart F, foreign tax credit and Form 5471) before you choose the holding structure, not after.

Payroll and employer cost

  • Employer social security is roughly 30–32% of gross salary, comprising common contingencies (23.6%), unemployment, wage guarantee fund, vocational training, an occupational-accident rate that varies by activity, and the intergenerational equity mechanism. Employees contribute around 6.5%.
  • Contributions are capped at a maximum monthly base (roughly €4,900 in 2025), so the marginal cost of senior hires falls, though a new solidarity levy is phasing in above the cap.
  • Salaries are typically quoted as an annual gross across 14 payments: twelve monthly plus two pagas extras, often in July and December. A U.S. offer letter quoting a monthly figure will be read as one fourteenth of the year, not one twelfth. Get this right or your first offer will be accidentally 17% low.
  • Personal income tax (IRPF) is withheld at progressive rates from roughly 19% to 47%+, with the top band varying by Autonomous Community.
  • The "Beckham Law" inbound regime taxes qualifying relocated employees at a flat 24% on Spanish employment income up to €600,000 for six years, and exempts most non-Spanish income. The Startups Law widened it to remote workers and to the employee's spouse and children. It is the single most effective lever for moving U.S. staff to Spain, and it must be elected within six months of registration.

Other taxes to price in

  • IBI: annual municipal property tax on owned real estate
  • IAE: business activity tax; exempt for the first two years and for turnover under €1M
  • ICIO: municipal construction tax, up to about 4% of the works budget
  • ITP / AJD: transfer tax on second-hand property (roughly 6–11%, set regionally) or stamp duty (roughly 0.5–1.5%) on VAT-taxed purchases
  • Plusvalía municipal: municipal tax on the increase in urban land value, payable on transfer
  • Plastic packaging tax: €0.45/kg on non-recycled plastic in non-reusable packaging
  • Transfer pricing documentation, plus Form 232 disclosing related-party transactions; assume every intercompany charge from the U.S. parent will be examined

Incentives worth chasing

  • R&D and innovation credits are among the best in the OECD: 25% of R&D spend (42% above a rolling average), 17% on dedicated researcher payroll, 12% on technological innovation, and they can be monetized for cash if you have no tax to shelter, which matters enormously for a loss-making first three years.
  • Patent box: up to a 60% reduction on qualifying IP income.
  • CDTI grants and soft loans for R&D projects; ENISA participative loans; ICO credit lines.
  • Regional aid and the PERTE programs (electric vehicles, semiconductors, agri-food, renewable hydrogen) funded through EU recovery money.
  • Social security rebates for hiring in certain regions and categories.
Because you chose: Basque Country / Navarre

Different corporate tax code entirely, with a headline rate near the national one but notably richer R&D and investment credits and its own filing calendar. For a research-intensive or capital-intensive operation this can be the deciding factor between regions, so model it properly rather than assuming Spanish national rates.

Because you chose: Canary Islands

The Zona Especial Canaria offers a 4% corporate tax rate, the lowest in the EU, for qualifying entities that meet minimum investment and local-hiring commitments within two years. Combine with the Reserva para Inversiones en Canarias and IGIC at 7% instead of 21% IVA. The trade-off is distance from mainland markets and a tax frontier on goods.

Because you chose: Andalusia / Extremadura

These are assisted areas on Spain's EU regional aid map, which permits higher-intensity capital grants than Madrid or Catalonia can legally offer. Combined with lower wage levels and land costs, the effective incentive package can be worth more than a headline tax rate difference. Open the conversation with the regional agency before you shortlist sites, because aid generally cannot be granted for a project already begun.

Because you chose: R&D / engineering

Get a binding informe motivado from the Ministry of Science certifying your projects as qualifying R&D. It is optional but it makes the credit effectively audit-proof, and it is what separates firms that claim the credit confidently from firms that provision against it. Stack it with the researcher payroll credit and the patent box.

Because you chose: Plant

Three plant-specific items: import VAT deferral (available if you file monthly; otherwise you finance 21% of every import for a quarter), free zones and customs warehousing at Barcelona, Cádiz, Vigo, Seville, Santander and the Canaries, and CBAM, the EU carbon border adjustment, which attaches financial obligations to imports of steel, aluminium, cement, fertilizer, hydrogen and electricity. If your inputs are on that list, CBAM is a line item in your landed cost model, not a compliance footnote.

Because you chose: Shared services / back office

A captive service center charging the U.S. parent is a transfer pricing exercise first. Expect a cost-plus arrangement with a defensible markup, contemporaneous documentation, and an intercompany services agreement signed before the first invoice. Spanish tax inspectors examine inbound service centers routinely.

Renting commercial space: how Spanish leases actually work

Non-residential leases are arrendamientos para uso distinto de vivienda under the Urban Leases Act. Unlike residential tenancies, they are governed almost entirely by freedom of contract: there is no statutory minimum term and very few protections implied in your favour. What the lease says is what you get, which cuts both ways.

Term and break rights

Typical structure is 5 or 10 years with an obligado cumplimiento, a mandatory minimum period, often 1 to 3 years, during which you cannot walk without paying out the balance. Negotiate the break option and its penalty formula explicitly; silence favours the landlord.

Rent, VAT and withholding

Rent is quoted in €/m² per month. Add 21% IVA. Separately, a business tenant must generally withhold 19% of the rent and remit it to the tax agency (Forms 115/180). You pay the landlord 81% plus VAT, not 100%. Landlords know this; new foreign tenants often do not, and pay twice.

Deposits and guarantees

A legal deposit of two months' rent is mandatory and must be lodged with the regional housing authority. On top, landlords will ask a newly formed foreign-owned company for a bank guarantee (aval bancario) of 6 to 12 months' rent, or a U.S. parent-company guarantee. Bank avals are cash-collateralized in practice, so budget the working capital.

Indexation and outgoings

Annual rent review is normally tied to CPI (IPC) or a negotiated index. Confirm who bears IBI property tax, community charges, insurance and structural repairs. Spanish leases commonly push all of these to the tenant, but only if the contract says so.

Due diligence before you sign or buy

  • Nota simple from the Land Registry: confirms ownership, mortgages, easements and embargoes. Non-negotiable, costs almost nothing, and is the first thing any Spanish lawyer pulls.
  • Cédula urbanística from the municipality: confirms the zoning actually permits your intended use. A building being "industrial" in the listing is not the same as the plot being zoned for your specific activity.
  • Licence transferability: check whether the existing activity licence covers what you will do and whether it transfers, or whether you restart the permitting clock.
  • First occupancy licence, energy performance certificate, technical building inspection where required.
  • Outstanding community charges and IBI: these attach to the property, and you can inherit them.
Land and offer, brokerage practice. Commercial agency is less regulated than in the U.S. and the same broker may quietly represent both sides. Appoint your own tenant representative, insist on a written fee basis, and expect an initial reservation document (documento de reserva) with a deposit before heads of terms. Under a contrato de arras penitenciales, a buyer who walks forfeits the deposit and a seller who walks repays double. Real money, not a formality.
Because you chose: Office

Office stock is graded A/B/C and clusters sharply: in Madrid, Paseo de la Castellana and the CBD command the top rents with far cheaper space in the A-1 and Méndez Álvaro corridors; in Barcelona, the 22@ district in Poblenou is the tech and creative cluster. Prime rents run roughly €30–45/m²/month in Madrid's CBD and €25–32 in central Barcelona, with secondary cities materially lower. Treat these as order-of-magnitude and verify current quotes, as the market moves.

Start in serviced or coworking space. Regus/IWG, WeWork, Utopicus and Cloudworks all let you take a registered business address and desks on a monthly contract with no aval, no deposit, no activity licence and no fit-out. It converts a 6-month property project into a same-week decision, and buys you time to learn the market before committing to a 5-year lease.

Because you chose: A small team

At under ten people, signing a conventional lease is almost always the wrong call. A flexible workspace contract avoids the bank guarantee entirely, which for a newly incorporated foreign-owned entity with no Spanish credit history is otherwise the hardest term to negotiate.

Because you chose: Plant

Industrial space means a nave industrial in a polígono industrial (industrial estate). Key moves:

  • Buy versus lease: leasing is faster and preserves capital; buying is common for heavy process plants and unlocks certain regional capital grants tied to fixed asset investment.
  • Verify utility capacity in writing before signing: electrical connection capacity in kW, gas, water supply and permitted discharge volumes. A site that cannot get the power you need is worthless at any rent, and grid connection queues in popular industrial zones are the most common cause of slipped production dates.
  • Check clear height, floor load rating, dock doors, yard depth and turning circles against your equipment spec, not against the listing photos.
  • Commission a soil report. Under RD 9/2005, potentially contaminating activities carry soil status obligations, and liability for historic contamination can follow the site. A baseline study protects you on the way in and on the way out.
  • Publicly developed industrial land (through regional land agencies such as SEPES) is often cheaper and comes pre-permitted for industrial use.
Because you chose: Warehouse / distribution

Prime logistics clusters are the Madrid A-2 / Corredor del Henares ring, the Zona Franca and Penedès areas around Barcelona, and the Valencia port hinterland. Prime rents are roughly €6–8/m²/month, with Madrid and Barcelona at the top. Vacancy in the best submarkets is low, so pre-lets and build-to-suit are normal rather than exotic.

Because you chose: Mediterranean corridor

Catalonia, Valencia and Murcia give you Spain's densest industrial base, the Port of Valencia (the largest container port in the Mediterranean by throughput) and the Port of Barcelona, plus the best road links to France. Two counterweights: Catalonia's property transfer taxes sit at the high end of the national range, and the eastern seaboard carries real flood and water-scarcity risk. The October 2024 Valencia floods hit industrial estates directly. Get a flood-zone check on any shortlisted site.

Because you chose: Madrid / central Spain

Madrid is the radial centre of the road and high-speed rail network, which makes it the natural national distribution point and the easiest place to reach every other city in a day. It is also the most expensive labour and property market in Spain, and it is landlocked, so every import arrives by road from a coastal port. Castilla-La Mancha and Castilla y León offer much cheaper land within trucking distance of the capital.

Because you chose: Northern Spain

The north (Basque Country, Navarre, Aragón, Galicia) is Spain's advanced manufacturing heartland, with deep automotive, machine tool, steel and aerospace supply chains and a genuinely skilled industrial workforce. You pay for it in higher wages and tighter labour availability than the south, and the terrain makes internal logistics slower than a map suggests.

Currency

  • Spain uses the euro. There are no exchange controls: capital moves freely into and out of Spain and across the EU. What exists is reporting, not permission.
  • Translation and transaction exposure are real. EUR/USD has moved through wide ranges in recent years; a Spanish subsidiary's euro earnings translate into consolidated dollar results at rates you do not control. Set a hedging policy before revenue starts, not after the first bad quarter.
  • Natural hedging first. Matching euro revenue against euro costs (local payroll, local suppliers, euro-denominated debt) removes more exposure more cheaply than any forward contract. A plant that sells into Europe is naturally hedged; a plant that exports to the U.S. is not.
  • For residual exposure, forwards and options through your Spanish or U.S. bank are standard. Intercompany funding should be documented at arm's-length rates to survive transfer pricing review on both sides.

Reporting obligations that surprise people

  • Bank of Spain ETE return: a Spanish-resident entity with foreign assets or liabilities above €1M must report them annually; higher balances trigger quarterly or monthly filing. Your intercompany loan from the U.S. parent counts.
  • Form D-1A: declare the inbound foreign investment to the Investment Registry within one month.
  • Cash limits. Payments of €1,000 or more between businesses or professionals cannot legally be made in cash (€10,000 for non-resident individuals). Cross-border movements of €10,000+ must be declared. Spain is serious about this and the penalties are percentage-based.

How money actually moves

  • SEPA transfers and IBANs, not checks. Paper checks are effectively extinct in Spanish business; instant SEPA credit transfers settle in seconds.
  • Direct debit, or domiciliación bancaria, is the default for recurring payments: utilities, rent, social security, taxes. The tax agency and Social Security expect to pull from your account on schedule. Get the mandates set up early; a failed direct debit on a tax payment produces a surcharge automatically.
  • Bizum is a bank-consortium instant payment system tied to mobile numbers, used by a large share of the adult population and increasingly by merchants. If you sell to consumers, it belongs in your checkout alongside cards.
  • Card penetration is high and contactless is universal. Cash persists in small hospitality and rural retail but is declining fast.
  • A Spanish IBAN is practically necessary. SEPA rules prohibit IBAN discrimination, but in day-to-day reality the tax agency, Social Security and many counterparties are far smoother with a domestic account. Do not try to run a Spanish entity from a U.S. bank account.

Payment terms and the collection problem

This is the single most underestimated operating difference. Spanish law caps B2B payment terms at 60 days from delivery (30 days absent agreement, 30 days for the public sector). Actual behaviour is looser: late payment is common, culturally tolerated, and enforcement has historically been weak because suppliers are reluctant to sue customers they want to keep. A U.S. firm modelling 30-day DSO will find its Spanish working capital requirement is materially higher than planned. Build the real collection cycle into your cash forecast, run credit checks (Informa, Axesor, Iberinform) on new customers, and consider credit insurance; Spain has a deep and competitively priced market for it.

Invoicing and e-invoicing

  • Invoices to public bodies must already go electronically through the FACe platform.
  • Mandatory B2B e-invoicing is coming under the Crea y Crece law, phased by company size once the implementing regulation is finalized.
  • Separately, the anti-fraud invoicing software rules (Verifactu) require billing systems to be certified, tamper-evident and, in the Verifactu mode, to transmit records to the tax agency. If you are choosing or configuring an ERP for Spain, check compliance before you buy, because retrofitting a U.S. billing system is far more expensive than selecting a compliant one.

Local transactions and commercial practice

  • Notaries are the gatekeepers of significant transactions. Property, corporate acts and powers of attorney all pass through them. This front-loads cost and time but makes the resulting title and corporate record exceptionally reliable. The flip side of the formalism is that you rarely litigate about what was actually agreed.
  • Contracts are shorter, and the Civil and Commercial Codes fill the gaps. A U.S.-style 120-page agreement signals distrust. Much of what U.S. counsel drafts explicitly is already implied by statute, and some of what U.S. counsel drafts is unenforceable here, notably broad waivers of statutory protections and at-will termination language.
  • Public procurement is a distinct discipline with formal tendering, classification requirements for larger contracts, and heavy use of UTE consortia. Do not treat it as an extension of commercial selling.
  • Consumer protection is strict: two-year legal conformity guarantee on goods, 14-day withdrawal on distance sales, and aggressive enforcement of unfair contract terms.
  • Relationship precedes transaction. Deals close after a meal more often than after a deck. See the operating-culture section below. This is not a soft point, it is a sales cycle length assumption.
Because you chose: Plant

Supplier payment terms in Spanish industrial supply chains often stretch beyond the legal cap in practice, and you will be on both sides of it. Two levers: confirming (reverse factoring, extremely common in Spain and offered by every major bank) lets your suppliers get paid early against your credit; and factoring your own receivables converts the long collection cycle into working capital. Also note energy price volatility: industrial electricity costs swing hard, and PPAs or on-site solar self-consumption are now mainstream ways to fix that input.

Because you chose: Office

Your currency exposure is mostly a cost-base exposure: euro salaries and euro rent funded from dollar revenue. That is a translation problem, not a transaction problem, and it is usually managed at the treasury level with a rolling hedge on forecast euro outflows rather than deal by deal.

Because you chose: Sales & commercial

Decide early whether you invoice customers in euros from the Spanish entity or in dollars from the U.S. parent. Euro invoicing wins deals, because it moves the FX risk off the customer, but puts it on you, and it has VAT, transfer pricing and permanent establishment consequences. Model all three before your first quote goes out, because changing it later is a customer-facing conversation.

Because you chose: 50 or more people

At this headcount your monthly euro payroll is a predictable, material outflow and is worth hedging systematically. A rolling layered forward program on 6 to 12 months of forecast payroll is the standard approach, and it removes most of the volatility from your Spanish cost base.

Employment law: the biggest gap from U.S. practice

There is no at-will employment in Spain. Every dismissal needs a legally recognized cause, delivered in writing with specified facts and dates. If a court finds the dismissal unfair, statutory compensation is 33 days' pay per year of service, capped at 24 months (higher accrual for service before 2012). A dismissal for objective economic or organizational reasons carries 20 days per year, capped at 12 months. This is the number that most often breaks a U.S. headcount model: exits are expensive, contractual and slow. Hire more carefully and use probation periods deliberately.
  • Collective bargaining agreements bind you whether or not you sign them. Sectoral and provincial convenios colectivos set minimum pay scales, job classifications, working hours, overtime rules and leave for your industry in your province. You cannot contract below them. Identify the applicable convenio before you build a compensation model. This is step one of Spanish workforce planning, and U.S. firms routinely discover it in month three.
  • Permanent contracts are the default. The 2021 labour reform sharply restricted fixed-term contracts to genuine production surges and substitutions; misuse converts the employee to permanent status and draws fines.
  • Probation periods: up to 6 months for qualified technical staff, 2 months otherwise (3 in companies under 25 employees). Use them, because after that the 33-day clock is running.
  • Working time: 40 hours per week averaged annually, 12 hours' minimum rest between shifts, overtime capped at 80 hours a year. Daily time registration is mandatory for every employee and records are retained four years. A statutory reduction of the working week to 37.5 hours has been debated and may return, so track it.
  • A minimum of 30 calendar days of paid holiday per year, plus 14 public holidays (national, regional and two local). August is functionally a closed month across much of Spanish business.
  • Employee representation: personnel delegates from 6 employees, a full works council (comité de empresa) at 50, with information and consultation rights over restructuring, working time and significant organizational change.
  • Remote work above 30% of working time over a three-month reference period requires a written agreement, and the employer must provide equipment and cover the associated costs.
  • Generous statutory leave: 16+ weeks of fully paid birth and childcare leave for each parent, paid at 100% by Social Security rather than by you.

Moving U.S. staff to Spain

  • Highly Qualified Professional permit under the Entrepreneurs' Law, processed by a dedicated large-company unit with fast statutory decision windows. This is the standard route for transferring senior staff.
  • Intra-Company Transfer (ICT) permit for moving existing employees within the group.
  • Digital nomad / international teleworker visa for staff who will remain employed by the U.S. parent while living in Spain, often paired with the 24% Beckham tax regime.
  • The general work permit route exists but is subject to a labour market test and is markedly slower.
  • The real-estate "golden visa" was abolished in 2025. If an adviser still pitches it, their information is out of date.
  • Short business trips run on the Schengen 90-in-180 rule, now administered through the EU's biometric Entry/Exit System. Track cumulative days for frequent travellers; the system now counts them automatically and overstays are enforced.

Working rhythm and business culture

  • The day is shifted later, not longer. Meetings commonly start at 9:30–10:00; lunch is the main meal, taken from roughly 14:00; dinner from 21:00. A 12:00 lunch invitation reads as strange, and an 8:00 a.m. call reads as inconsiderate.
  • Lunch is a business institution, not a break to be optimized. A two-hour lunch with a counterpart is doing the deal, not delaying it.
  • Relationships precede contracts. Expect a longer front end to the sales cycle and more in-person time than the equivalent U.S. deal. The time is recovered later in loyalty and dispute avoidance.
  • Hierarchy is real but communication is warm. Decisions concentrate at the top; interruption and animated disagreement in meetings signal engagement rather than conflict.
  • Language. Spanish is universal in business, English is common among younger professionals in Madrid and Barcelona and thinner elsewhere. Catalan, Basque and Galician are co-official in their territories and carry genuine political and commercial weight. In Catalonia, consumer-facing labelling and signage obligations in Catalan are enforced. Localize by region, not by country.
  • Regional identity is not decorative. Treating Spain as one homogeneous market is the most common strategic error U.S. firms make here.
Because you chose: Plant

Industrial sites are where Spanish labour relations are most institutionalized. Expect a strong sectoral convenio (the provincial metal industry agreements are the archetype), active union presence (CCOO and UGT are the two majors) and a works council that will be consulted on shift patterns, safety and restructuring. This is manageable and predictable once you accept it as the operating model. Engage the works council early and genuinely; U.S. plants that treat consultation as a formality generate the disputes they were trying to avoid. Occupational risk prevention (PRL) obligations are also substantially heavier on an industrial site, with a mandatory prevention service, documented risk assessments and health surveillance.

Because you chose: Office

Office roles typically fall under an oficinas y despachos or sector-specific convenio, which is lighter than industrial agreements but still sets minimum terms. Hybrid work is widely expected in Madrid and Barcelona professional roles, and remember that formalizing it above the 30% threshold triggers a written remote work agreement and an expense allowance.

Because you chose: R&D / engineering

Spain's engineering talent is strong and comparatively inexpensive against U.S. benchmarks, which is much of the business case. The constraint is not supply but retention: Madrid and Barcelona now host many international tech centres competing for the same people, and Spanish employees weigh schedule, holiday and stability heavily against pure cash. An offer that wins in Austin may lose in Barcelona for reasons that have nothing to do with base salary.

Because you chose: Shared services / back office

Spain is a well-established nearshore location for multilingual shared services. The pool of staff with Spanish, English, French, Portuguese, German and Italian is deep and Málaga, Barcelona and Madrid all host large captive centres. Two planning points: the time zone overlaps Europe fully but the U.S. only partially, which is precisely the constraint discussed in the culture section below; and attrition-driven staffing models built on at-will hiring do not transfer: a shared services centre sized on the assumption of easy exits will be expensive in Spain.

Because you chose: 50 or more people

Crossing 50 employees triggers a cluster of obligations at once: works council elections, a registered equality plan negotiated with employee representatives, a pay register and pay audit, an LGTBI protocol, and an internal whistleblowing channel. Each has its own deadline and its own penalty for absence. Put all of them on the plan before you cross the threshold, not after.

Ghemawat Framework

CAGE: Analyzing Distance

Cultural, Administrative, Geographic and Economic distance between Spain and the United States. Spain is closer than most U.S. firms assume on geography and further than they assume on administration and culture, and it is never one uniform market.

CAGE Distance Key Spain vs. U.S. differences Business Implication
Cultural Distance
  • Languages: Spanish, Catalan, Basque, Galician, Valencian and Aranese, plus many regional dialects. The U.S. is English-dominant but highly diverse through immigration.
  • Collectivism vs. individualism: Spain tends to be more relationship-based; the U.S. more individualistic, speed-oriented and direct.
  • Daily rhythm: later meals and some siesta-style afternoon breaks; the U.S. business rhythm is faster and more compressed.
  • History and identity: deeper Roman, Catholic, regional and Mediterranean roots. The U.S. turns 250 in 2026, with immigration shaping a younger culture.
  • Regional localization matters.
  • Trust-building may matter more than speed.
  • Marketing, hiring, signage, service and relationships may need to vary by region.
Administrative Distance
  • Political system: Spain is a constitutional monarchy and parliamentary democracy; the U.S. is a federal republic.
  • Regional structure: no states, but 17 Autonomous Communities, 2 Autonomous Cities, 50 provinces and 8,000+ municipalities, against 50 states, D.C. and territories.
  • EU / euro layer: Spain is in the European Union and uses the euro; the U.S. uses the dollar and sits outside the EU system.
  • Regional variation: the Basque Country and Navarre hold unique fiscal powers. U.S. states also vary in taxes, labor rules, incentives and regulation.
  • Corruption perception (CPI): Spain 55/100, rank 49/182; the U.S. 64/100, rank 29/182.
  • Spain is smaller, but not uniform.
  • Rules operate at national, EU, regional and local levels.
  • Region choice affects permits, taxes, incentives, public-sector relationships and timelines.
  • Due diligence matters, given slightly higher perceived public-sector corruption risk.
Geographic Distance
  • Scale, movement and connectivity: Spain is about the size of California at roughly 505,370 km², with Europe's largest high-speed rail network, second globally after China, at roughly 3,973 km. The U.S. is about 9.8 million km², over 19× larger, and relies far more on highways, domestic flights, freight rail and regional hubs.
  • Terrain and mountains: Spain is one of Europe's most mountainous countries, second only to Switzerland, with the Meseta Central plateau shaping the interior. The U.S. has the Great Plains, the Appalachians in the east, and the Rockies and Sierra Nevada in the west.
  • Mainland, islands and territories: Spain also includes the Balearic Islands, Canary Islands, Ceuta and Melilla. The U.S. has a large contiguous mainland plus Alaska, Hawaii and its territories.
  • Climate and water risk: drought and flooding on the Mediterranean seaboard are live operational risks, as the October 2024 Valencia floods showed.
  • Shorter distances and rail coverage make national reach and coordination easier after entry than the U.S. equivalent.
  • Terrain still constrains logistics, so map distance overstates real reach.
  • "Smaller" does not mean one simple market: islands and territories add logistics, tax-frontier and regional planning needs.
  • Site selection should include flood-zone and water-availability checks.
Economic Distance
  • Economic scale: Spain $1.89T GDP / $38,040 per capita; U.S. $29.18T GDP / $85,810 per capita in 2025.
  • Economic drivers: Spain leans on services and tourism, creating seasonality risk; the U.S. is driven by consumer spending, technology, innovation, finance and large regional clusters.
  • Valencia example: tourism is over 15.5% of regional GDP, manufacturing 19%, and agri-food created nearly 50,000 jobs in 2023.
  • Income and safety net: lower average income and wages but stronger public benefits, including universal healthcare and public pensions. The U.S. has higher purchasing power but higher wealth inequality.
  • Labor market: Spanish unemployment around 10.5%, youth unemployment around 23%. The U.S. labor market is more fluid, with faster hiring and firing cycles.
  • The U.S. offers more scale and purchasing power.
  • Spain requires pricing and demand adaptation by income, region and seasonality.
  • Hiring strategy needs local fit around labor protections, youth unemployment and regional talent.
  • Spain remains attractive through lower labor costs, regional clusters and EU market access.
